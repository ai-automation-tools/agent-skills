# Myth and motif analysis

Two stories that share an element raise one question: **why**? There are three answers,
and the analysis has to argue for one with evidence:

| Explanation | Evidence that supports it |
|:---|:---|
| **Common inheritance** | Related languages and peoples, shared vocabulary for the element, a reconstructable earlier form (Indo-European studies, Semitic) |
| **Diffusion** (borrowing through contact) | Documented contact, trade or conquest; a plausible route and date; the borrower's version adapting the source's details |
| **Independent invention** | No plausible contact; the element arises from common human experience (floods, death, sunrise, kinship) |

"Both have a flood" supports none of them by itself.

## 1. Pin down the versions

Name each story's actual source text or recorded telling, with its date and locus, before
comparing (the `sacred-texts` and `religious-narratives` approach). Comparative mythology has
a long record of comparing summaries of summaries; compare texts.

## 2. Classify with the standard tools

| Tool | What it does |
|:---|:---|
| **Aarne–Thompson–Uther (ATU) index** (Uther 2004) | Tale types: whole plots, numbered (ATU 510A: Cinderella) |
| **Thompson's Motif-Index of Folk-Literature** | Motifs: single elements, lettered and numbered (A1010: Deluge) |
| Regional and specialist indexes | Many traditions have their own; check before forcing a story into ATU |

A shared motif number is a starting point, not a conclusion.

## 3. Weigh the parallel

- **How specific?** A flood is common; a flood with a boat, birds sent out to find land, and
  a sacrifice afterwards (Gilgamesh XI and Genesis 8) is specific enough to argue
  connection.
- **How much differs?** List the differences as carefully as the shared elements.
- **What's the chronology?** Which version is attested first, and could the other have
  known it?
- **Is the parallel in the sources or in the summary?** Many famous "parallels" (Horus and
  Jesus, Mithras and Christmas) are much weaker or absent in the primary sources than in
  popular retellings.

## 4. Know the theories and their critiques

| Theory | Claim | Main critique |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Frazer, *The Golden Bough* | Dying-and-rising vegetation gods underlie many religions | Built from selective, decontextualized sources; the category is contested (J. Z. Smith) |
| Eliade | Myths enact sacred time and archetypal patterns | Universalizing; hard to test |
| Campbell, the monomyth | One hero's-journey pattern underlies hero stories | Fits by selection; ignores the stories that don't fit; little traction with scholars of the traditions |
| Lévi-Strauss, structuralism | Myths work through binary oppositions | Illuminating for some corpora; hard to falsify |
| Dumézil, trifunctional hypothesis | Indo-European myth reflects sovereignty, force, fertility | Influential but disputed in scope |
| Witzel, historical comparative mythology | Laurasian and Gondwana mythologies with deep-time descent | Ambitious; contested methodology |

Report a theory as a theory with its critics. "Joseph Campbell argued…" is fine; "all hero
myths follow the hero's journey" is not.

## 5. Write it

The answer to "why do these share X" first, with its confidence. Then each version with its
source, the shared and differing elements in a table, the evidence for each explanation, and
the theories brought to bear, attributed.

Living traditions' sacred stories are scripture to their communities. Analyzing them is
fine; describing them as "just myths" is not.
